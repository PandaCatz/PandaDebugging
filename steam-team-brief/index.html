Splitting the money without losing friends

How Steam actually pays, what structure the team needs, and what keeps everyone honest. Researched against Valve's docs and game-law sources, 12 August 2026.

The fact that shapes everything: Valve pays each game's revenue to exactly one partner account, into a bank account whose holder name matches the partner's legal identification. From Valve's docs: payments are "associated one to one with partner and account. Any splits or further payments that need to be made are your responsibility."

Sources: Steamworks Reporting & Payments FAQ · Steam Direct

So there is no "Steam splits it for us" option. Whoever signs with Steam receives 100% of the money, and everything after that is contracts between us. That's why the structure question matters.

Getting on Steam at all: any adult can publish — company or individual. Onboarding is identity verification (a few business days), a tax questionnaire (W-9 for US, W-8BEN for non-US), and banking details. The Steam Direct fee is $100 per game — non-refundable, recouped once the game passes $1,000 adjusted gross. Two timing gates people miss: a mandatory 30-day wait between paying the fee and your first release, and a "coming soon" store page that must be publicly visible at least 2 weeks before launch (realistically months, for wishlists).

Three ways to structure it

The default trap

Do nothing

A group making profit together with no entity and no paperwork is a general partnership by default — no filing needed, a handshake creates it. Every member is personally liable for what any member does, and courts fill the missing terms with statutory defaults: equal profit shares and equal management authority regardless of who actually did the work. This is the structure we have today.

Option A · cheapest

One person publishes as an individual

One of us signs with Steam personally; everyone else signs a revenue-share contract with them. Fine for a free or jam game. Downsides: all revenue is that person's taxable income (they then issue 1099s), the Steam account and the game live or die with one human, and every payout is a trust exercise.

Option B · recommended

Multi-member LLC

The LLC signs Steamworks, owns the game, and receives the money. The operating agreement is the honesty mechanism: ownership %, vesting, decision rules, exits. Taxes pass through — each member gets a K-1 and pays tax on their share. Cost: roughly $50–$500 state filing plus small annual fees; file in the state where the managing member lives (Delaware buys nothing at our scale). Then an EIN (free, IRS website) and a business bank account.

Option C · skip

Corporation

Only worth the overhead if we're raising investment. We're not. Skip.

Fixed split or earned split?

Two workable models — pick one deliberately:

Fixed percentages with vesting. Agree the splits now, but each share is earned monthly or per milestone across the remaining dev period (startups use 4-year/1-year-cliff; game teams usually vest to ship date). Simple to run; renegotiate only if roles change.

Dynamic split ("Slicing Pie"). Nobody guesses percentages up front — a ledger tracks contributions (hours × an agreed rate × a risk multiplier; cash counts extra) and the split at any moment is your slice of the ledger, frozen at launch. Fairest when nobody knows who'll end up doing the work; costs real bookkeeping discipline every week.

What actually keeps everyone honest

Not the entity itself — the paperwork plus visibility:

  • A signed agreement now, before more work goes in. Even one page: splits, decision rules (pricing, discounts, DLC), what happens when someone leaves, dies, or ghosts, and how a departing share gets bought out.
  • Define "revenue" precisely. Gross? After Valve's cut? After expenses? Game lawyers call this the one definition that shapes every payment that follows — pin it, and ban vague terms like "fair share."
  • IP assignment, signed before work continues. IP does not transfer automatically — each contributor owns their own code, art, and music until they assign it to the entity in writing. Without it, any one person can block the release or walk off with assets in the shipped game.
  • Vesting (or the dynamic ledger above). Someone who bails three months in doesn't keep a full share of a game the rest finished.
  • Everyone reads the numbers at the source. Steamworks supports multiple user logins with per-permission access — give every member View Financials (partner level) or View Financial Info (per app: sales and download data). Data comes from Valve, not from a screenshot. Same visibility on the business bank account.
  • Name who holds "Actual Authority." Valve assigns one Steamworks role that alone can touch bank details, tax info, and legal agreements — and admins can only grant permissions they hold themselves. Write into the agreement who holds it and what they may do unilaterally (nothing involving money is a good default).
  • Distributions on a schedule, expenses first. Quarterly: revenue − expenses − an agreed holdback (taxes, refunds, marketing, post-launch content) → distribute per the agreement. Writing the holdback % down now kills most future arguments.
  • Splits with the reasoning recorded. Equal is fine if everyone agrees — but write down why, because renegotiating later with money on the table is where teams die.

The path from here

  1. Now: sign a short collaboration agreement — splits (fixed-with-vesting or dynamic), IP assignment, decision rules, exit terms. Template-level is fine to start.
  2. When the game is clearly shipping: form the LLC, get the EIN, open the business bank account.
  3. Early, not launch week: LLC signs Steamworks and pays the $100 — verification takes days, the fee starts a 30-day release clock, and the "coming soon" page needs ≥ 2 weeks (months, realistically) of wishlist time. Add every member as a user with financial visibility; decide who holds Actual Authority.
  4. Before release: 1–2 hours of a games/business attorney on the operating agreement, one hour of an accountant for the K-1 setup. (The "bring a lawyer in at release" instinct was right — this is what they're for, and it's a few hundred dollars, not a retainer.)
  5. After launch: distributions on the agreed schedule, holdback first, everyone watching the same Steamworks dashboard.

Reality checks

If any member is outside the US, a US multi-member LLC gets genuinely painful: the LLC must withhold US tax on foreign members' shares (up to 37% for individuals unless a treaty cuts it) and file extra IRS forms on a calendar. The usual fix: entity in one country, everyone else contracts with it on revenue-share agreements. That's the one case where the professional comes in early, not at release.

On building our own store: that isn't dodging the 30% — it's founding a second company (payment processing, global VAT remittance, fraud, refunds, hosting, and zero built-in audience). The realistic version of "be like Epic" is publishing on Epic's store too: self-publishing costs $100/app, Epic's cut is 12%, and since mid-2025 developers keep 100% of the first $1M net revenue per product per year (resets each January; Unreal projects also get the engine royalty cut from 5% to 3.5% above $1M for day-one EGS releases). itch.io alongside (10% default, adjustable 0–100%) is the other low-effort channel. Multi-store costs little; a storefront of our own is not a launch-week decision.

General information, not legal advice — the operating agreement is the one document worth paying a professional to review.
